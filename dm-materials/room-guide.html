Room-by-Room Guide

A linear chain of rooms, each with a door on the far side and more than one way to reach it.
The Hook. Before the hayride even leaves the lot, one PC's little brother — tagging along after being told to stay home — is standing right there when everything goes wrong. Name him at the table if you haven't already; the party needs someone specific to be chasing. Every room from here on can drop a small sign that he passed through recently and not long ago.
The Barker. Since the cast is thin on NPCs, one voice carries the whole dimension — a Ringmaster/Barker who's never physically present, only heard through speakers, funhouse mirrors, and cue cards. He taunts, lies, and occasionally helps if it amuses him. Whatever he's saying, he's always selling the same pitch — have fun, enjoy it, make the most of it while it lasts — classic carny patter that only curdles once his real motive comes out at the end. He shows himself, in person, only at the very end, and he isn't alone when he does.
0Cold Open — The Hayride

Whoever's dad ends up driving gets them there, the little brother in the back seat working through yo-yo tricks he's clearly been practicing, trying to get someone — anyone — to watch. Old Dick runs the hayride — has for decades, long enough that the driving parent remembers him from their own childhood, and he hasn't seemed to age in the meantime. The trailer's a horse trailer, and it locks from the outside. The tractor pulls them into a barn bigger than it looked from the field, and the door rolls shut behind them.

Old Dick. A decades-long town fixture who doesn't seem to have aged. Whether he's complicit, oblivious, or something in between is yours to decide at the table.

Obstacle: no obstacle — this is the hook that gets them moving. Set into the barn's back wall is a ticket window, dark the whole ride, now lit and humming. A gloved hand extends a single ticket through it. Only the little brother seems drawn to it. He takes it, and the window stops being a frame — it takes him whole. Everything goes still. Old Dick is gone. The window's still open, warm and lit, and it's the way through.

1Carny Games

Stepping through the window doesn't lead to a hallway — it leads straight into a garish carnival lobby, ticket booth and all, three game stalls lit up and running like they never stopped. A hand-painted sign arches over the ticket booth: WONDERYEAR TRAVELING CARNIVAL — "The Good Times Don't Have to End." The window seals shut behind the last person through it. A bag of 10 tokens is waiting on the counter — one token, one play, at any stall, spent however the party likes.

Obstacle: the exit door at the far wall has three empty keyholes. Each stall has its own huckster running a rigged game, and each one can still be won more than one way.

Ring the BellKey 1

A huckster leans on an enormous sledgehammer, inviting people to try their luck — then hands over a comically small one the second someone actually pays. The puck's already resting right at the tipping point.

  • Common SenseThe tiny hammer — or even a bare hand — is plenty. The puck's already primed to tip; the swap was only ever meant to make it look impossible.
  • BrainsSpot the rigged counterweight bolted behind the tower and disable it instead of hitting anything at all.
  • BrawnsIgnore the tiny hammer and just hit it — fist, elbow, whatever's on hand. It was never really about force.
  • Wildcard / LuckyA player narrates a plan and it just works — no roll, just conviction.
Duck PondKey 2

A huckster hands out a fishing pole with a magnet that's obviously too weak for the job — not that it would matter. The real key is tied to a duck chained to the bottom of the pool, and the padlock key hangs on a chain around his own neck.

  • BrainsA careful look reveals the water's churning on its own — something's moving underneath — and one duck doesn't drift with the current like the rest. It's chained down.
  • BrawnsBreak the chain by force, or improvise a way to snap it with whatever's on hand.
  • Wildcard / HooliganPickpocket the padlock key off the huckster's neck — or, the darker option, push him into the water and take it while the piranhas are busy.
  • HiddenTip the whole pond over. It drains, but exposes an anaconda coiled around the chain's anchor point — dealing with it is the real cost of this shortcut.
Complication: reaching into the water without checking it first risks a piranha bite (treat as a Deep Cut). Some of the other ducks carry real prizes instead of decoys — a single-shot pistol, a bottle labeled "Health Potion" that works exactly as advertised, a rabbit's foot (reroll one failed check), a pocket mirror (useful later in the Hall of Mirrors), and a clown nose (blend in as one of the Big Top's performers for a scene). A token pulls one at random instead of going for the key.
The WheelKey 3

A big prize wheel, its leather flapper clicking against pegs as it spins down. The huckster leans on a hidden foot pedal every time it stops short of the key wedge — a tell visible in his posture if anyone's watching closely.

The Six Wedges:

  • The KeyGold paint, chipped bare from decades of hands not quite reaching it. Landing here wins Key 3 outright, no further roll needed.
  • A PrizeA crude cartoon of a grinning duck points off toward the pond stall. Pull blind from the leftover prize pool (pistol, "Health Potion," rabbit's foot, pocket mirror, clown nose).
  • NothingJust a flat, faded gray — the only wedge with nothing painted on it at all. The huckster shrugs like he expected it.
  • Spin AgainA hand-drawn arrow looping back on itself. Free respin, no token cost.
  • PossessedA pair of eyes, crossed out and scratched in later than the rest — like someone added it after learning the hard way. The wheel keeps spinning after it should've stopped, and so do you: Possessed, for a beat.
  • The Wheel BitesSplintered wood, dark stains nobody's bothered to sand out. The flapper snaps back hard enough to catch flesh: Injured, that hand.
  • BrainsWatching a spin — his demo, or someone else's turn, free of charge — reveals the tell: he leans into the brake right before the key wedge. Time a shout or a slap on the counter to break his rhythm at that exact moment.
  • BrawnsSkip the tell entirely — just grab the wheel and force it past the brake.
  • Backflips / SkaterboyFast enough to flick the flapper itself past the sticking point right as it hesitates.
  • HiddenTrace the pedal's cable under the counter and jam or cut it before ever spinning — no roll needed once found.
Complication: beating the rig only guarantees landing where they're aiming; a party that never spots the tell is just gambling on all six wedges.
Complication: failing a game doesn't lock the party out — it just costs a token, and the Barker gets a little nastier over the speakers. As always, enough Brawns can skip the room entirely by tearing the door off its frame instead of collecting all three keys — messier, and worth an Injury roll.
Secret: underneath each stall counter is a small brass plaque engraved with the name of whoever "won" that game last — nobody in the party. A fourth plaque is screwed into the floor beside the exit door, blank, waiting to be filled in.
Sign of him: a half-melted candy wrapper of his favorite brand sits on one of the stall shelves — still warm.
2The Hall of Mirrors

Every mirror shows a slightly wrong version of the viewer — aged, injured, or doing something they haven't done yet. The layout never looks quite the same twice.

Obstacle: the mirrors reposition themselves whenever nobody's looking directly at them, turning the hall into a maze that can't be memorized — only whoever last looked away knows it moved.

Secret: each mirror is being carried by an undead minion, silent and still as long as it's watched. Breaking a mirror, or catching one mid-shift, exposes the thing underneath — and it will grab or block whoever exposed it to keep the maze closed.
  • BrainsKeep something on every mirror at once — a phone propped to record, mirrors angled to watch other mirrors, the party splitting to cross-watch — so nothing can move unobserved. Freezes the maze in place.
  • Common SenseThe minions aren't silent — footsteps, dragging, breathing. Follow the sound instead of the sight to find the gaps without needing to catch anything moving.
  • Backflips / SkaterboyCatch a mirror mid-shift and slip through the half-second gap before it reseals — fast enough that nothing gets a grip.
  • Brawns / HooliganSmash straight through. Shatters the mirror, exposes the minion carrying it, and turns the puzzle into a straight scramble to the door.
  • Urban ExplorerKeep a steady, unhurried pace through a space that clearly doesn't want to be moved through — hesitation is what lets a minion catch up, not speed.
Complication: an exposed minion doesn't chase far or fight hard — it just grabs. Getting caught triggers Possessed — the minion has you for a beat instead of you having yourself.
Sign of him: for just a flash, one mirror shows him running past in the reflection — not in the room, only in the glass.
3The Talent Show

Past the mirrors, a narrow hallway dead-ends into a cramped prep room — cracked mirrors, a costume rack of ill-fitting outfits, a call sheet with the party's names already on it in handwriting that isn't theirs. One more name is listed below theirs — his — with "up next" written beside it in ink that's still wet.

The Usher. A female clown in smudged greasepaint meets them here, both eyes drifting slightly out of sync, never quite settling on whoever she's talking to. She explains the rules like it's the most normal thing in the world: they're on next, the judges decide who leaves, and the trapdoor behind the judges' table only opens for a majority. As she turns to go, she shouts back toward the prep room, irritated: "Where is that last judge?! That damned Professor better be on time!"

Through the wings, they catch the tail end of his act — a yo-yo trick he's clearly been practicing for weeks, the string singing through a run of impossible loops, landing it clean. The dead audience roars like it's the best thing they've ever seen. He's grinning in a way nobody's seen on him in months.

Obstacle: onstage, only two judges sit behind the table — a third chair beside them is empty, noticeably newer than the other two, and nobody explains why. With just the Starlet and the Wreck voting, the trapdoor needs both of them to agree. If the third chair gets filled in the party's favor, they only need one.

The X. A straight lift from a certain reality singing competition, except the buzzer's real: each judge has a giant lit-up X button set into the table in front of them. The first X hit arms a ring of spikes under the stage floor with a click everyone in the room hears. The second X — both judges hitting it together, or one after the other while the first is still armed — fires them, killing whoever's performing on the spot. No roll, no negotiating. It takes something genuinely bad to earn an X, not just an average performance — how bad is bad enough is a table-time call.
  • HiddenThe trapdoor's release lever sits in plain sight under the judges' table — while both are locked on whoever's performing, someone else can trip it directly, no votes needed, as long as nobody misses their cue to hold attention.
The StarletVote 1

An eyeless, glittering mannequin doing her best impression of the judge who cries at every audition — gushing praise one second, dabbing nonexistent tears the next. Wants a show, not a skill; it takes real, sustained awfulness to get her anywhere near that button.

  • Beauty / Theater KidA full dramatic number — monologue, song, dance — sold with total commitment.
  • DancingA genuine dance number wins her over on technique alone.
  • Class ClownLean into an exaggerated comedy bit instead of grace — she rewards commitment to a bit just as much as elegance.
The WreckVote 2

Hulking, scarred, missing an arm — the judge who tells you the truth whether you wanted it or not, if "the truth" also came with a body count. Wants danger and spectacle; boring him is the fastest way to see his remaining hand drift toward that button.

  • BrawnsA feat of real strength — breaking, lifting, or throwing something the room clearly doesn't want moved.
  • Backflips / SkaterboyA dangerous acrobatic stunt, the riskier the better.
  • Explosives / HooliganA loud, dangerous flourish — thrilling if it works, worth an Injury roll if it doesn't.
The Third Chair. A closet just off the prep room turns up the real Professor, passed out cold, coat and monocle within reach. The chair gets filled one of two ways: impersonate him — take the coat, take the seat, and it counts as a vote in the party's favor with no performance needed — or perform well enough in front of the Starlet or the Wreck that the chair pulls a PC in on its own, the same way it's claimed every judge before them. Getting caught impersonating counts the same as being pulled in: if a vote locks in while someone's seated there, they're on the panel permanently.

Escaping the chair:

  • BrawnsSomeone physically hauls them out of the chair before the next vote locks in.
  • Common SenseCast a vote so lopsided and unfair it offends the panel's sense of fairness, getting them ejected on the spot.
  • Beauty / Class ClownCharm the chair itself into losing interest and letting go.
Complication: failing to win a majority doesn't end the act — the Usher just says "again" and the show restarts, colder and stranger each time. Enough Brawns can force the trapdoor without any votes, but doing so turns every judge and the dead audience against the party at once — a much bigger Overwhelmed than skipping any other room's puzzle.
4The Big Top

Optional: cut if running short on time — the party can go straight from the Talent Show to the Chancel.

The next room is a sagging circus tent packed with a floor show that never stops. Acrobats loop the same dead swing forever, a strongman lifts the same rusted barbell, a tightrope walker missteps and catches herself in the exact same place every time. Nothing here breathes.

Obstacle: there's no door at the far end — the only way out is a tear at the peak of the tent, high above the ring, with the whole show happening directly underneath it.

The Ringleader. A gaunt figure in a rotted ringmaster's coat stands center ring, conducting the performers with slow, precise gestures like a puppeteer. He doesn't chase — he directs. Disrupt the loop too obviously and he turns his baton, and every performer's attention, toward whoever did it.
  • Backflips / Skaterboy / Marching BandTime a leap onto a trapeze mid-swing and ride its arc up to the tear, matching the acrobats' loop instead of fighting it — the same rhythm sense that keeps a step in time on a field works here.
  • BrawnsSkip the show entirely and climb the support rigging or tent pole hand over hand.
  • Brains / Movie BuffClock the exact repeating loop and slip through a stagehand's catwalk that the performers habitually ignore.
  • Beauty / Theater Kid / Class ClownJoin the act as a "volunteer" — sell it well enough that the Ringleader directs the cannon, lift, or trapeze to feature you, launching you toward the tear as part of the show.
  • Wildcard / Explosives / HooliganRig something loud to break the loop and open a gap in the crowd — fast, but the noise turns the Ringleader's baton straight at whoever caused it.
  • Doomsday PrepperAlready has rope, a harness, or a grappling hook stashed in their bag for exactly this kind of moment — skips the need to improvise a climb at all.
  • HiddenThe curtained wagon at the ring's edge hides a maintenance ladder leading straight to the tear — climbing it skips the performance entirely, but being caught anywhere near it draws the Ringleader's full attention with no warning shot.
Complication: if the Ringleader notices, every performer converges on the interruption at once — a fast, overwhelming grab rather than a fight. Getting caught risks Injured, or Overwhelmed if other statuses are already active.
Secret: the wagon is where the Ringleader keeps his next act prepped before debut. It's empty right now — but the smell inside is unmistakably a child's, not a performer's.
Sign of him: the Ringleader keeps glancing toward the same curtained wagon, like he's guarding something small inside it. It's empty if anyone checks — but not for long.
5The Chancel

Optional: cut if running short on time — the party can go straight from the Big Top to the Finale.

A ritual chamber — pews facing an altar. The final door is behind it, but the altar demands a trade: something be left behind before it opens.

Obstacle: an item, a memory, or a lie admitted aloud — the altar wants something given up.

  • BrainsFind the loophole in the ritual's wording — it wants something given up, not necessarily something valuable.
  • Beauty / Class ClownTalk the room into accepting a performance or confession as the trade instead of an object.
  • Common Sense / Thrift Store KidSacrifice a prop/decoy item deliberately brought along earlier — rewards foresight from earlier rooms, and a Thrift Store Kid always has one more thing in their pockets than anyone expects.
  • Poker FaceLie about what's actually being given up, and sell it so convincingly the altar can't tell the con from the trade.
  • HiddenA leftover key, token, or plaque scavenged from an earlier room satisfies the trade just as well as something personal, if anyone thought to keep one — the altar doesn't check where an object came from, only that it's really given up.
Complication: if nobody's willing to give anything up, this is a natural spot for Despair or Injured (grief) to trigger instead of a hard fail.
Secret: the altar isn't testing generosity, it's testing whether the party's learned the dimension's rule — everything here demands a trade eventually. Anyone already carrying an active Injury or Status can offer that instead, and the altar accepts it immediately, no argument needed.
Sign of him: the altar wants "something you can't get back" — and for the first time, giving something up feels less like a prop and more like it might actually cost them him.
6Finale — The Threshold Home

The exit door is real, past a gallery of cages lining both walls — a freak show's worth of exhibits nobody's fed or spoken to in years. At the far end, past all of them, one last cage holds the real attraction: the Barker, finally showing himself — a gaunt figure in a faded, striped jacket with long tails, the fabric worn soft and thin from decades of wear, an old tin megaphone tucked under one arm like it's never once left his side. Up close, he's unmistakably a teenager, no older than seventeen — it's just his face that doesn't match, lined and worn in ways a kid's face shouldn't be, like every year he never should've had to carry settled into the skin instead of the rest of him. He isn't alone. The little brother stands beside him in the same cage, calm in a way that isn't right, already dressed in a smaller version of the same jacket — except it hangs loose and childish on him, obviously borrowed, like a kid playing dress-up. He's most of the way through becoming the dimension's newest attraction, the same way the judges and the mirror minions got their start.

The Cages. Four more line the walls, silent for now. The Conjoined Twins share more than skin at this point — they finish each other's sentences and share a single scream. The Human Pincushion, covered in old rusted pins that never seem to hurt him, keeps pulling fresh ones from his own arm to offer like gifts. The Sword Swallower brings the same blade back up over and over, throat long since ruined, never actually dying from it. The Fat Man is swollen far past human proportion, jovial and warm in a way that's deeply wrong from inside a cage. None of them move while the Barker's talking — the whole room stays perfectly still for this part of the show.

Obstacle: no new attribute gate. This isn't a puzzle to solve, it's a rescue to finish. The cages keep the party from just rushing in — the Barker and the boy are out of reach until something changes. The Barker wants a trade, an audience, maybe a replacement — not a fight. Every earlier room's choice — what they left in the Chancel, who's Bleeding, who's Overwhelmed, whether anyone got stuck in the Third Chair and had to be pulled free — determines how hard this is, and how much of him is still in there to bring home.

The Barker, finally speaking plainly: "You don't remember what it feels like anymore. Not really. That first Halloween where the fear was the whole point, before you got tired, before you got embarrassed of wanting things this much. He still has it. Whole. Untouched. And what's waiting for him back home — two houses, a suitcase, a version of 'family' that doesn't exist anymore — I'm not stealing anything from him. I'm sparing him." (turning to the boy) "You don't have to go back to any of that. Not the fighting. Not the packing. Not choosing who gets you for Christmas. Here, no one can leave, and no one wants to! Stay. Just stay." (back to the party) "So — enjoy yourselves. Really. However this goes, however long you've got left in you — enjoy it. It doesn't last. Ask your dad."

If he accepts: the Barker holds out the megaphone — the one he's been carrying the entire time — and the boy takes it. The yo-yo slips out of his other hand and rolls away; he doesn't reach for it. The Barker's face breaks into relief, then something like ecstasy — he lifts off the ground and comes apart in the air, screaming "I'm finally free!!" like it's the best thing that's ever happened to him. In the same instant, every cage in the room springs open at once — the Twins, the Pincushion, the Swallower, the Fat Man, and the one that held the Barker and the boy. By the time the pieces stop falling, the jacket on the boy fits right — perfectly tailored, like it was always his. That's the tell that it's too late to just talk him down. What stands up afterward has his face and his voice, but everything else — the cadence, the patter, the total certainty — is the Barker's. This is the real final form, loose in a room full of four other things that just got loose with it; beating it is the only way left to bring any of him home.

Secret: the coat is the binding, not the boy. If it comes off before the transformation completes, it partially unravels — the further along he is, the harder the coat fights to stay on.
  • HiddenGetting the coat off him — by force, by trick, or by talking him into taking it off himself — undoes more of what's been done than beating the Barker in a straight fight ever would.

If he falls: the whole tent collapses around them at once — the ring, the judges' stage, the game stalls, everything folding into canvas and dark together. They have to fight their way out from under the falling fabric, and when they finally claw free, they're standing in the open field where the barn once stood. No barn. No tent. No ticket window. Just a field, at night, like nothing ever happened — except for Old Dick, collapsed in the grass a few yards off, dead, but at peace in a way he never looked while he was running that ride. He's clutching a photo of a woman none of them recognize, worn soft from years of handling. He doesn't explain, and maybe he can't anymore. Whatever became of everyone else left inside is anyone's guess.
